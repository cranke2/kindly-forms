7 mistakes families make on the Attendance Allowance form

The form is long, and the DWP decides mostly on what you write. These are the mistakes that most often lead to a refusal or the lower rate, and what to write instead.

1. Describing a good day

Many parents play things down, and on a good day they really do cope. But the DWP needs to know what happens across a normal week, bad days included.

Instead of: "Mum can get dressed by herself."
Try: "On about four days a week Mum can't do up buttons or put on shoes because of arthritis in her hands. On those days I dress her, which takes about 20 minutes. On better days she dresses alone but it takes 40 minutes and she has to sit down to rest."

2. Writing what they can do instead of the help they need

The test is the help they need, even if nobody gives it. If they manage alone but slowly, in pain or unsafely, that counts.

Instead of: "Dad lives alone and manages."
Try: "Dad lives alone, but he has fallen twice getting out of the bath this year. He now only washes at the sink and needs someone there to help him in and out of the bath safely."

3. Leaving out how often and how long

"Needs help with the toilet" doesn't tell the decision maker much. Say how many times a day or night, how long each time takes and what goes wrong.

Try: "She needs to use the toilet two or three times a night. Each time she needs steadying to get out of bed and back, which takes about 10 minutes. Without help she has fallen."

4. Thinking walking problems don't count

Attendance Allowance has no mobility part, so families often leave walking out. But help needed because of poor mobility, such as getting to the toilet, getting out of a chair, or being watched on the stairs, is exactly what counts.

5. Forgetting supervision

It isn't only hands-on help. If someone needs to keep an eye on them to stop them coming to harm, for example because of dementia, falls, forgetting the cooker, wandering or seizures, describe that too.

6. Writing about how the money will be spent

Families sometimes explain what care they plan to pay for. The form is about needs, not spending, and the money can be used for anything. Stick to what help is needed and why.

7. Leaving out the statement from someone who knows them

The form has space for a statement from someone who knows them, such as a GP, nurse, carer or relative. A short, specific statement from someone who sees the problems first-hand adds real weight. A week's care diary attached to the form helps too.

One more thing: call before you start

If you ask for the form by calling 0800 731 0122 and return it within six weeks, the claim can be paid from the date of the call rather than the day the form arrives.
